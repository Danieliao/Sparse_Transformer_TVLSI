1. More comprehensive comparisons are needed to establish the advantages of RRLC. The current manuscript mainly compares RRLC with CSC, COO, and conventional RLC. The authors are encouraged to further compare RRLC with bitmap-based sparse encoding schemes [1] and sparse-packing approaches such as Mentha [2], in terms of compression efficiency, metadata overhead, hardware complexity, and supported dataflow.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have extended the comparison in two ways: a quantitative comparison of RRLC against the bitmap, COO, and CSC encodings under a common storage model, and a qualitative comparison of the two cited works.

Quantitative comparison (Fig. 3(b), Section II-B). Fig. 3 now has two panels. Panel (a) is the previous figure. Panel (b) evaluates the bitmap, COO, and CSC encodings under the same matrices, sparsity statistics, and bit accounting as RRLC, on the feed-forward weight matrices of ViT-Base in INT8 from 60% to 90% sparsity. The new paragraph in Section II-B explains the result through the metadata cost per non-zero of each format and gives the crossover: RRLC overtakes the bitmap from 73% sparsity onward (3.4x against 3.1x at 80%, 5.1x against 4.4x at 90%), while below that point the bitmap is more compact but outside the 75% to 90% weight-sparsity range the chip targets. The same paragraph states why storage is not the reason RRLC was chosen: the alternatives yield their non-zeros serially, whereas the RRLC decoder issues N non-zeros to N multiplier lanes in every cycle from a single memory word. Section II-A now names the bitmap alongside COO, CSC, and RLC with the reason each resists parallel consumption. On hardware complexity, the only measured number we can give is for our own decoder, 0.16% of the core area (Table III). For the other formats the manuscript describes the address-generation structure each requires, since their papers do not report an isolated decoder cost.

We compare RRLC to [1] and [2] in Section II-B, new paragraph.

Reference [1] is a CNN edge accelerator (GlobalFoundries 22FDX, 0.5 mm2, 51 kB SRAM, 26 GOPS, 4.4 TOPS/W on sparse AlexNet) whose central contribution is a programmable dataflow with flexible loop orders. Its encoding is chosen to survive arbitrary loop orders and is in fact two schemes. Weights are stored run-length coded (CSS) in DRAM and L2, precisely because, as the authors state, run-length coding is more efficient at the high sparsities of pruned weights. Each tile is then decompressed into an uncompressed, bitmask-tagged L1 so that it remains random-accessible under any loop order. Activations are stored dense with a bitmask in DRAM, and only the transfer across the off-chip boundary skips zeros. The authors' own Table IV shows the bitmask outperforming a run-length code only at the roughly 50% sparsity typical of activations, and they note that its uncompressed nature requires more off-chip storage. Reference [1] therefore corroborates two of our positions: run-length encoding for highly sparse weights, and bitmask for moderately sparse activations. The problem that [1] avoids, parallel consumption of a run-length stream at the MAC level, is exactly the problem RRLC solves. Reference [1] avoids it by decompressing into an uncompressed L1, whereas RRLC keeps the operand compressed up to the multiplier. The paragraph in Section II-B states this contrast. Because [1] is a 26 GOPS CNN accelerator, we have not added it to the chip comparison of Table V.

Reference [2], Mentha, is not an operand encoding but a utilization technique for two-dimensional systolic arrays. Rows of the sparse operand whose non-zeros do not overlap are merged into one packed row along the non-common dimension, each element tagged with its source row, so that the array processes fewer and denser rows. A reconfigured PE with several accumulators (1.28x area and 1.21x power in [2]) steers each product to the right output row by the tag, and the packed result is un-packed afterwards. Three properties follow, which the new paragraph states. First, the gain is bounded by the packing threshold, i.e., the number of accumulators a PE holds (2 to 8 in [2]), not by the sparsity. Second, the columns that no merged row fills still occupy systolic slots. The PE gates its multiplier but the zero is not skipped at zero cycle cost. Third, a packing pass is required, either an offline graph-coloring pass or an on-line windowed queue that packs less well. The row-wise product needs none of this: every non-zero input element addresses its own weight row, so zeros are skipped natively and the utilization does not depend on a packing threshold. Mentha quantifies its compression as the reduction in row count after packing, not as stored bytes, so it cannot be placed on the storage axis of Fig. 3. Since [2] is a simulated FP32 systolic framework rather than a silicon implementation, it likewise does not enter Table V.

2. The capability of the taped-out prototype should be more clearly distinguished from that of the proposed on-line RRLC encoder extension. The fabricated prototype does not contain an on-line RRLC encoder, and dynamically generated activations are pre-encoded by the host, while the newly added encoder is only a synthesized extension. Therefore, the meaning of "full-transformer" or "end-to-end" execution should be clarified to avoid implying that on-chip encoding of intermediate activations has been demonstrated in silicon.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We agree, and we have removed every formulation that could be read as a claim of on-chip encoding. The scope of the silicon demonstration is now stated in the same terms at every place where a whole-model statement is made: every GEMM and every non-linear operator of the transformer block executes on the chip. Weights are encoded into RRLC once, offline. Intermediate activations are returned to the host, which re-encodes them before the next operator is issued. The on-chip encoder is a synthesized and verified extension that is not part of the taped-out chip.

The specific changes are:

- Section II-B: after the statement that weight encoding is performed offline, we now add that, in the fabricated prototype, activations are likewise supplied in RRLC form, encoded by the host between layers.
- Section III-C3: the paragraph on the on-line encoder now ends with the statement that the encoder is not part of the taped-out chip and that, in the prototype, the re-encoding of intermediate activations is completed by the host.
- Section IV-A: the measurement description now states how the models were run: the GEMM and PPE operators are mapped on the chip with weights and activations supplied in RRLC form, the pruned weights are encoded once offline, and intermediate activations return to the host, which re-encodes them for the next operator. All throughput, power, and energy values are stated to be measured on the chip only.

The terms "end-to-end" and "full transformer" no longer appear in the description of this work.

3. The novelty of the Softmax design should be positioned more clearly. The log-sum-exp formulation, LUT-based exponential approximation, and leading-one-detection-based logarithm are closely related to existing Softmax hardware implementations [3]. The more distinctive contribution may lie in the integration of Softmax with the Accumulator Memory, Softmax Memory, and subsequent GEMM dataflow. This system-level contribution should be more clearly distinguished from the approximation algorithm itself.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We agree with this assessment and have repositioned the softmax contribution accordingly. The approximation algorithm is now attributed entirely to prior work at every place where it is mentioned, and the contribution of this work is stated to be the integration of that pipeline into the GEMM dataflow.

On the citation: the method of [3] (Zhu et al., TCAS-II 2020) is the journal version of the conference design by the same group (Wang et al., APCCAS 2018), which the manuscript already cites for the log-sum-exp formulation, the exponential look-up, and the leading-one-detect logarithm. The formulation, the exponential and logarithm approximations, and the pipeline are the same in both. Reference [3] adds the precision-adjustable variant. We have therefore kept the existing citation, which is the origin of the method we adopt, and we state explicitly that the approximation is adopted from it.

The specific changes are:

- Abstract, Section I, and Section V: the softmax is no longer described as a linearization contributed by this work but as an approximation adopted from prior work and embedded in the matrix-multiplication pipeline, where the scores are partially processed as they leave the RMMPE and the remaining processing overlaps the next matrix multiplication.
- Section III-B3: a new opening paragraph states the system-level contribution before the drain-by-drain description: the cited arithmetic is integrated into the GEMM datapath rather than instantiated as a standalone special-function unit, reusing the GEMM adder tree plus a single max register, holding only 3-bit clipped differences in Softmax Memory, and overlapping the Softmax Memory drains with the next GEMM.
